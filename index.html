Executive summary

Stable headline rates can hide unequal labor-market risk.

Demand shock

Job losses reduce spending

When workers lose income, household demand falls. Businesses then face lower sales, cut production, and may lay off more workers, deepening a downturn.

Skills mismatch

Education changes exposure

People without a high school diploma repeatedly show the highest unemployment, while bachelor’s degree holders show the lowest rates across the series.

Purchasing power

Inflation shifts winners and losers

Inflation hurts cash holders, fixed-income households, and workers whose wages lag; fixed-rate borrowers and asset owners may benefit as dollars lose value.

Part 1 · The unemployment rate

February 2025 showed stability, not equality.

The overall unemployment rate was 4.1% in February 2025. But demographic rates ranged from 3.8% for White workers and adult women to 6.0% for Black workers, 5.2% for Hispanic workers, and 12.9% for teenagers.

Official unemployment also excludes discouraged workers who are not actively searching, making underemployment and hidden unemployment important context for interpreting the rate.

Education gap

More education consistently correlates with lower unemployment.

In downturns, all education groups are affected, but not equally. Workers without a high school diploma reached 14.9% unemployment in 2010 and 11.7% in 2020. Bachelor’s degree holders peaked far lower at 4.7% in 2010 and 4.8% in 2020. Duration of unemployment

Long-term unemployment is where recessions leave scars.

The count of workers unemployed for 27 weeks or more surged to 6.415 million in 2010. Long spells can reduce callbacks, weaken skills, strain families, and make reemployment harder. What the duration data shows

  • Short spells rise quickly when layoffs accelerate.
  • Long-term unemployment peaks later as weak demand persists.
  • Extended joblessness adds financial stress and psychological costs for families.

Part 2 · Price inflation

Inflation slowed in February 2025, but household pain was uneven.

CPI-U rose 0.2% in February 2025 after a 0.5% increase in January, while the 12-month change was 2.8%. Motor vehicle insurance and food proteins rose sharply, while fuel oil and energy commodities declined.
